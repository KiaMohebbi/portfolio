The CVaR efficient frontier, the factor risk cap, and the leverage check (prices the choices; no leverage)

Logged 2026-07-19.

Traces the whole CVaR frontier the STARR tangency point sits on, adds an optional per-factor risk-contribution cap, and checks whether leverage ever pays. The notebook prices the choices rather than picking one. Leverage is not used, but not for the reason expected, and the record says so.

Question

The engine picks one portfolio: the STARR tangency point, reward per unit of CVaR, solved by the Rockafellar-Uryasev representation with Dinkelbach iteration so that each step is a linear program. This notebook traced the whole curve that point sits on, so the risk/return exchange rate is visible rather than implied, added an optional cap on how much of total risk a single factor may contribute, and asked whether leverage would ever pay.

Design (no new modelling)

  • A CVaR frontier, not a mean-variance frontier. Every point is produced by the same regime-mixture scenario generator and the same CVaR objective the engine uses. A Markowitz curve would describe a different, un-validated model (single-regime, Gaussian, thin-tailed) and would mean optimizing against a model the pipeline rejected. Each frontier point maximizes strategic expected return subject to a CVaR budget, long-only with the per-asset cap; the budget is linear under Rockafellar-Uryasev, so each point is one linear program. The tangency portfolio is one point on this curve. Volatility is reported, never optimized.
  • Frozen inputs, holdout untouched. Covariance, expected returns, generator, regime model, degrees of freedom, and the cap reused verbatim; estimated as of the pre-holdout recommendation date.
  • Where the recommendation actually sits. Two exact, additive risk decompositions of the engine’s chosen portfolio: per asset on CVaR, and per factor on variance, the latter documented as a proxy used only for attribution because it decomposes exactly through the factor model at every weight. Latent factors pooled on purpose; negative shares are hedges, not bugs.
  • The per-factor cap. A constraint on the fraction of total portfolio risk one factor contributes, not on exposure or weight. It is quadratic and indefinite, hence non-convex, so it is solved by sequential linearization with the method disclosed: the achieved share is always recomputed exactly, the requested cap is never printed as attained, and points where the cap could not be met are drawn hollow. The cap’s cost is measured at matched risk. A crisis-pure scenario set supplies each point’s crisis CVaR and crisis multiple.
  • Leverage reality check. A kinked capital allocation line with a borrowing rate above the lending rate, so there are two tangencies with a fully invested band between them; the borrowing rate is a knob.

Outcome (the notebook does not pick a point; it prices the choices)

  • Higher return requires higher CVaR. The frontier is monotone by construction and the marginal exchange rate falls moving right: the first units of return are cheap, the last expensive.
  • The recommendation was a rates bet wearing a diversified portfolio’s clothing. The rates factor dominated the chosen portfolio’s risk share despite many names, visible only because the factor model decomposes risk by source. Counting names is not a diversification measure.
  • The cap is a diversification constraint, bought not harvested. It costs return at every matched risk budget, so it cannot be return-chasing. Whether the concentration it removes is worth the cost is a risk-preference judgment. The record forbids re-running the sweep and picking the cap with the best return.
  • Strategic expected returns are not promises. The return axis inherits the un-backtestable consensus level; the risk axis rests on the validated risk model. Trust the risk axis more than the return axis; differences of a few basis points between adjacent points are noise in the expected returns.
  • Moving up the frontier raises crisis exposure faster than the blended number shows.
  • Leverage: the expected answer did not survive contact with the numbers, and it is reported as it landed. The notebook was set up to confirm that no frontier point out-earns an assumed retail borrowing rate. A few cap-stacked corner points do clear it in expectation, so the blanket claim is false and is not asserted. The practical conclusion holds for a different and better-supported reason: those points sit far outside the region this portfolio operates in (with crisis CVaR higher still), levering them scales the crisis CVaR that forces liquidation, and borrowing at a contractual, certain rate to harvest an estimated, un-backtestable return is a worse trade than the nominal margin suggests. Return should come from moving up the frontier, not from borrowing. The borrowing-rate sweep reports the crossover above which leverage stops paying anywhere.

Decision

The optimizer stayed as built. The production instance of this machinery is the live allocation: it re-traces the frontier on all history through the latest bar, applies the rates cap, and delivers the frontier point at a chosen monthly CVaR budget (a moderate point, deliberately not the min-risk corner, and a knob), with leverage verified as not applied at each strike rather than assumed. The standing target’s committed forecast is published on the Home page: predicted CVaR at the four-week horizon and annualized volatility. The leverage and sizing layer itself remains designed but not built, which is also why the conservatism multiplier ships at its raw setting.

Where it led

  • The single-use holdout found the benchmark beat the STARR allocator on return, risk-adjusted return, and drawdown, a real strike against the allocator on that sample.
  • The mu-sensitivity study took the budgeted frontier point as the production decision object and measured how far plausible expected-return error moves it.
  • The crash-arrival blind spot is a position-level sizing question; the overlay frontier characterized it with adoption left open.

After July: rf in the objective and the delivered rule (2026-11)

On 2026-11-16 the risk-free rate (rf), zero in the code until then, entered the STARR objective in the numerator and the denominator. rf is the 4-month Treasury bill from the U.S. Treasury par yield curve.

On 2026-11-17 the numerator moved to the CVaR’s 4-week horizon and the displayed STARR became annualized with sqrt(13), labeled excess over T-bills; the weights did not change.

The delivered rule, adopted 2026-11-17, is the max-STARR tangency over the engine’s universe minus SHY, BKLN and UUP (per-asset cap 25%, dRATE risk share at most 0.35), mixed with T-bills to a 2.5% total 4-week CVaR95 budget, no borrowing. A stop rule keeps the previous weights if the tangency sits at a frontier end, the dRATE cap cannot be met or the solve fails. The previous rule, the capped frontier row nearest 2.5%, is now the shadow, tracked beside the delivered portfolio in the private records; it is not shown on this site.

The owner chose it on 2026-11-17 after seeing the July-snapshot tables, including the instability result: a 1-point move in one asset’s expected return moves a single tangency weight by up to 25 points. There is no out-of-sample evidence (the single-use holdout is spent) and no performance claim. On the engine’s own July numbers the delivered mix had a lower expected return (6.12% against 6.68%) and a higher CVaR95 (2.50% against 2.28%) than the row it replaced.

Rebalancing follows rule B (prereg amendments 1 and 2): every 4th Friday, if any holding, the T-bill sleeve included, is more than 5 percentage points from its standing target, the whole portfolio goes back to target; otherwise the weights drift. Re-strikes come only on the first such Friday at least 6 calendar months after the last, and the 5-point test then uses the new target. The go-live strike is the one unconditional move; the shadow follows the same rule.

Model of record

Drafted 2026-11-17. The July entries on the other research pages point here for “the Current Model page”, which this page replaces. The T-bill rate in the objective, the 4-week numerator and the delivered rule date from 2026-11 (see After July), after those entries; no July study scored them. The model behind the delivered portfolio, as the specification’s model-of-record sections state it:

  • Risk model. A weekly Student-t predictive. Its covariance is the 156-week equal-weight, single-regime hybrid factor covariance (observable factors plus the latent components that survive a random-matrix cut), scaled by the frozen V3 vol-state dial. The degrees of freedom were fit causally on an expanding pool of whitened residuals; the engine reads their 2026-07-17 freeze value, as two passages of the specification say, while its tail-shape passage says they continue to update causally: the records disagree.
  • Scenarios. The single-regime iterated Student-t generator (R-M1) draws the 4-week scenarios from that covariance; the regime mixture no longer has this job.
  • Expected returns. Sleeve level: the bond sleeve at its raw observable curve yield (the high-yield and emerging-market haircuts kept), the risky sleeve at one equity-premium level with the reverse-optimization tilt (kappa 0.25).
  • Objective, rate and delivered rule. STARR with the 4-month Treasury bill in the numerator and the denominator, at the 4-week horizon, and the delivered rule described in After July.
  • What the regime model still does. See Still in use.
  • Known limitation. Crash arrival is the model’s blind spot: trailing estimators lag an arrival by construction. The overlay policy aimed at it is characterized and its adoption is open; see Crash-arrival risk and the overlay frontier.
